*This is an opinion piece. It reflects TekkTech's editorial view on how to think about value, rather than a review of any specific product.*

Every year, new gadgets arrive with longer specification sheets and higher prices. Launch coverage focuses on what is new. But "new" and "worth it" are different questions, and the second is the one that matters when it is your money.

We think value comes down to a handful of qualities that rarely appear in a headline. Here is how we judge it.

## Cost per year, not cost on day one

A device's price only makes sense when you divide it by how long it will be genuinely useful. An expensive phone that receives security updates and runs well for six years can be better value than a cheap one that becomes sluggish or unsupported after two.

This is why we pay close attention to **published software support commitments**. A manufacturer that states clearly how long a device will receive updates is giving you information you can plan around. Vague promises are not the same thing.

## Usefulness you will notice every day

Many new features are impressive in a demonstration and forgotten within a week. The features that justify a price are the ones you will benefit from constantly: a battery that comfortably lasts your day, a screen that is easy to read, a keyboard you enjoy typing on, a camera that captures moments reliably rather than occasionally brilliantly.

When we assess value, we ask a simple question: **which of these improvements would you notice on an ordinary Tuesday?**

## Repairability and the price of accidents

A cracked screen or a tired battery should not be the end of a device's life. Gadgets that can be repaired at a reasonable cost — with available parts, documentation and service options — protect your investment. Ones that cannot are effectively more expensive than their price tag suggests, because an accident can mean starting again.

## Respect for the owner

Value also includes how a product treats you after you buy it. We think less of devices that:

- push advertising into paid products;
- require accounts or subscriptions for basic functions;
- lock features you have paid for behind ongoing fees;
- collect more personal data than their function needs.

None of this shows up on a specification sheet, but all of it affects whether you will be glad you bought something a year later.

## When paying more makes sense

None of this means cheaper is always better. Paying more can absolutely be worth it when it buys:

- substantially longer support or better build quality;
- a meaningful improvement in something you use constantly;
- accessibility features you rely on;
- a product that will hold its usefulness — and often its resale value — for longer.

The mistake is paying more for features you will not use, or for a newer model whose improvements over last year's are marginal for your needs.

## Our test for value

When we decide whether something is worth its price, we ask five questions:

1. How many years of genuine usefulness is it likely to provide?
2. Which improvements will I notice every day?
3. What happens if it breaks or the battery wears out?
4. Does it respect my time, attention and data?
5. Is there an older or simpler option that does what I actually need?

A gadget that answers these well is worth paying for — whatever its price. One that answers them poorly is expensive, even when it is on sale.
