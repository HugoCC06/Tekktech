Getting higher FPS on a gaming PC often seems to involve an unpleasant compromise: reduce the graphics settings and enjoy smoother gameplay, or keep everything looking beautiful and accept inconsistent performance. Fortunately, that is not always necessary.

Modern games include dozens of graphics options, and some have a much greater impact on performance than others. Lowering the right settings can deliver a noticeable improvement without making the game look dramatically worse.

The trick is to understand which options affect image quality, which consume significant graphics resources and which are worth leaving alone.

**For most PC gamers, the best starting point is to reduce demanding shadows, volumetric effects and ray tracing before sacrificing texture quality or resolution unnecessarily.** The ideal configuration still depends on your graphics card, monitor and the game itself.

## 1. Lower shadows before textures

Shadow quality is often a sensible place to start when a game struggles to maintain a consistent frame rate.

High-quality shadows can require substantial rendering work, particularly in games with complex environments, dynamic lighting and numerous moving objects.

Try changing shadow quality from Ultra to High or Medium. The visual difference can be relatively modest compared with the performance gained, although some games handle shadows better than others.

Textures are a different matter. They primarily affect the detail visible on surfaces such as clothing, buildings and landscapes. Reducing texture quality can make a game look noticeably worse, particularly when viewed up close.

If your graphics card has sufficient video memory, keeping textures on High while lowering shadows can provide a better balance.

## 2. Be selective with ray tracing

Ray tracing can improve lighting, reflections and shadows, but its performance cost varies significantly between games and hardware configurations.

If you are struggling to maintain your target frame rate, disabling ray tracing is one of the most effective settings to test in games where it is particularly demanding.

You do not necessarily need to disable every ray-traced effect. Some implementations are relatively affordable, while others can dramatically increase the rendering workload.

Compare the game with ray tracing enabled and disabled in the same location. If the improvement is substantial and the visual difference is acceptable, you have found a worthwhile trade-off.

## 3. Choose the right resolution

Resolution affects how many pixels your graphics card must render. Moving from 4K to 1440p or from 1440p to 1080p can substantially reduce the workload.

However, lowering resolution can make the image softer, especially on a monitor designed for a higher native resolution.

Before making that compromise, consider using a supported upscaling technology.

NVIDIA DLSS, AMD FidelityFX Super Resolution and Intel XeSS can reconstruct an image from a lower internal rendering resolution. Depending on the game, hardware and selected quality mode, they can improve performance while retaining more detail than a straightforward resolution reduction.

Start with a Quality preset if available. If performance remains insufficient, try Balanced. Performance modes can provide additional speed but may introduce more visible image degradation.

## 4. Use DLSS, FSR or XeSS where appropriate

Upscaling is often one of the most useful tools available to modern PC gamers.

These technologies are not identical, and their compatibility varies by game and graphics hardware. DLSS features generally target supported NVIDIA hardware, while FSR supports a broader range of configurations, depending on the version and implementation. XeSS support also depends on the game and available hardware.

The important point is to choose the right mode for your system rather than assuming the most aggressive option is always best.

A Quality setting can preserve a convincing image while reducing rendering costs. In a demanding game, that may be preferable to lowering several unrelated graphics settings.

## 5. Treat frame generation differently from ordinary FPS gains

Frame generation creates additional frames between conventionally rendered frames. Supported technologies can make movement appear smoother, particularly on high-refresh-rate displays.

However, generated frames are not the same as rendering every frame directly. Frame generation does not eliminate the underlying rendering workload, and it can introduce visual artefacts or additional latency.

It works best when the game already has a reasonably strong base frame rate. If performance is extremely low, improve the underlying rendering performance first.

For competitive games where responsiveness matters, a stable native frame rate may be preferable to a higher displayed FPS number produced through frame generation.

## 6. Adjust volumetric effects and reflections

Volumetric fog, clouds, lighting effects and reflections can be expensive, especially in visually complex games.

Reducing these settings from Ultra to High or Medium can sometimes produce useful gains without dramatically changing the overall presentation.

The effect depends on the game. Volumetric lighting may be central to a game's atmosphere, while screen-space reflections may be less noticeable during fast-paced action.

Change one setting at a time and compare the results in the same scene. That makes it easier to identify which adjustments deliver the best value.

## 7. Keep anti-aliasing and textures sensible

Anti-aliasing reduces jagged edges, particularly around thin objects and diagonal lines. Its performance cost depends on the technique used.

If your game offers modern temporal anti-aliasing or a supported upscaling option, compare those alternatives before disabling anti-aliasing entirely.

Texture quality deserves similar attention. High-resolution textures can consume substantial VRAM, but lowering them unnecessarily can make surfaces look flat and blurry.

If you experience stuttering or texture pop-in, test a lower texture setting to determine whether graphics memory is a bottleneck. Otherwise, retaining higher-quality textures can be worthwhile.

## 8. Reduce unnecessary background activity

Not every performance problem comes from graphics settings.

Browsers with many tabs, recording software, launchers and other applications can consume memory or CPU resources. Background tasks may also interrupt gameplay.

Close applications you do not need, check Task Manager for unusual CPU or memory usage, and avoid installing dubious optimisation utilities that promise instant performance improvements.

If a game is limited by CPU performance rather than GPU performance, lowering graphics quality may produce little improvement. Monitoring utilisation can help identify the actual bottleneck.

## 9. Use a sensible FPS limit

The highest possible frame rate is not always the best target.

If your system fluctuates wildly between high and low frame rates, a sensible cap can help create a more consistent experience. The right limit depends on your monitor, game and hardware.

A frame-rate cap can also reduce unnecessary GPU workload, heat and power consumption when the computer could otherwise render far more frames than you need.

Competitive players may prefer a different approach from people playing single-player games. Test the available options rather than assuming that an unlimited frame rate is always preferable.

## 10. How to find your ideal settings

Use a repeatable process rather than changing every option at once.

1. Start with the game's High preset.
2. Record the frame rate in a representative scene.
3. Reduce shadows, volumetric effects and ray tracing if needed.
4. Test a Quality upscaling mode where supported.
5. Adjust textures only if graphics memory or performance requires it.
6. Repeat the same test after each change.

Pay attention to frame-time consistency as well as average FPS. A game that averages a high frame rate but suffers frequent stutters may feel worse than one with a slightly lower but more stable result.

## The bottom line

You do not need to turn every graphics option to Low to get smoother PC gaming.

Start with expensive lighting effects, shadows and ray tracing. Use supported upscaling technologies where appropriate, preserve texture quality when your hardware allows it, and investigate CPU or memory bottlenecks before blaming the GPU.

The best settings are the ones that deliver a stable, responsive experience while preserving the visual details that matter to you. Make small, measured changes and let the results guide your decisions.
