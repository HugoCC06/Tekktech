Every productivity app promises to organise your life. The real cost of choosing the wrong one is rarely the subscription fee — it is the weeks spent moving notes, rebuilding projects and relearning habits when you eventually leave.

A free trial is your chance to avoid that. Most people waste it by clicking around the interface. This guide sets out a more deliberate approach: decide what you need, test it with real work, and check the things that are hard to change later.

## Start with the job, not the app

Before you install anything, write down the one or two problems you want solved. "Get organised" is too vague to test. Better examples:

- "I lose track of follow-ups after meetings."
- "My notes are spread across three apps and I can't find anything."
- "My team doesn't know who is responsible for what."

These statements become your test criteria. An app that is excellent at something you don't need is not a good app *for you*.

## Test with real work for at least a week

Demo projects always look tidy. Move a real, current piece of work into the app — a project with deadlines, messy notes and other people involved — and use it daily for at least a week.

Pay attention to friction:

1. **Capture.** How quickly can you add a task or note from your phone, your computer and your email? If capture is slow, you will stop doing it.
2. **Retrieval.** A week later, can you find what you saved? Test search with partial words and typos.
3. **Review.** Does the app make it easy to see what matters today, without rebuilding your view every morning?

If you find yourself working around the app, note it. Small annoyances in week one become reasons to quit in month three.

## Check that you can leave

This is the step most people skip, and the one that matters most in the long run. Before you commit, find out:

- **Can you export everything?** Look for open, documented formats such as Markdown, plain text, CSV or standard calendar files — not just a proprietary backup that only the same app can read.
- **What does an export actually contain?** Do one. Check whether attachments, dates, tags and links between items survive.
- **What happens if you stop paying?** Some apps keep your data readable; others lock it until you resubscribe.

An app that makes leaving difficult is asking for more trust than one that doesn't.

## Read the privacy information, then verify it

Productivity apps often hold sensitive material: meeting notes, plans, personal reminders. Read the privacy policy, and compare it with the app-store privacy summaries. Apple's App Store shows developer-provided privacy details, and Google Play has a Data safety section; both are completed by the developer, so treat them as claims to check rather than guarantees.

Useful questions include whether your content is encrypted, whether the provider can read it, whether it is used to train AI features, and where it is stored. Our guide to [assessing software privacy claims](/productivity/assess-software-privacy-claims/) goes into more detail.

## Work out the real price

Subscription pricing can be surprisingly hard to compare. Before deciding, calculate:

- the annual cost, including any features you will need in practice;
- the cost for everyone who will use it, if it is a team tool;
- whether essential features — such as offline access, sharing or exports — sit behind a higher tier.

Then ask whether a cheaper or free tool, used well, would solve your original problem. Often it would.

## Look for signs of longevity

You are investing in a tool you hope to use for years. Some evidence is worth looking for:

- a public changelog showing regular updates and bug fixes;
- clear documentation and a responsive support channel;
- a business model that makes sense — if a product is free with no obvious way of paying for itself, consider how it might change.

None of these guarantees that a product will survive, but together they give you a reasonable signal.

## A simple scorecard

At the end of your trial, rate the app against your own criteria:

| Criterion | Questions to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Solves my problem | Did it fix the issue I wrote down at the start? |
| Daily friction | Was capture and retrieval fast enough to keep using? |
| Portability | Could I export everything in a usable format? |
| Privacy | Am I comfortable with how my data is handled? |
| Cost | Is the realistic annual cost justified? |
| Longevity | Does the product look actively maintained? |

If an app scores poorly on portability or privacy, think hard before committing — those are the two problems that are hardest to fix later.

## The bottom line

The best productivity app is the one you will keep using, that respects your data, and that you can leave without losing your work. A disciplined one-week trial is the cheapest way to find it.
