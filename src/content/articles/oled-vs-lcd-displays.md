Whether you are buying a phone, a laptop, a monitor or a TV, you will usually choose between two families of display: **OLED** and **LCD**. Both can look excellent. They simply produce light in fundamentally different ways, and that leads to different strengths.

This comparison explains those differences and suggests which technology suits which kind of use. It describes the technologies in general terms; individual products vary widely, so always check reviews of the specific model you are considering.

## How each technology works

**LCD** (liquid crystal display) panels do not produce their own light. A **backlight** shines through a layer of liquid crystals that act like tiny shutters, plus colour filters. Better LCDs divide the backlight into zones that can dim independently — known as *local dimming* — and "mini-LED" designs use many small LEDs to create a large number of zones.

**OLED** (organic light-emitting diode) panels are *self-emissive*: every pixel produces its own light and can switch off completely. There is no separate backlight.

That single difference explains most of the comparison that follows.

## Contrast and black levels

Because an OLED pixel can turn fully off, OLED screens show true black next to bright highlights, giving them exceptional contrast. This is particularly noticeable in dark rooms and in films or games with lots of shadow detail.

LCDs always have some backlight leaking through, so blacks can look dark grey. Local dimming improves this considerably, but bright objects on dark backgrounds can produce a faint halo ("blooming") where a dimming zone is lit.

**Advantage:** OLED.

## Brightness

LCDs with strong backlights can be very bright, including across the whole screen at once, which helps in sunlit rooms. OLED brightness has improved, but many OLED panels limit brightness when large areas of the screen are bright, to manage heat and wear.

For HDR content, look for credible certifications rather than marketing terms. VESA's DisplayHDR programme, for example, defines tiered performance criteria for monitors.

**Advantage:** usually LCD, especially for very bright rooms — but it depends on the specific model.

## Burn-in and longevity

OLED materials age with use, and pixels that display the same bright, static content for long periods — such as taskbars, channel logos or game interfaces — can age unevenly, leaving a faint permanent image. Manufacturers use mitigation techniques such as pixel shifting and refresh cycles, and many people never notice a problem, but the risk is not zero.

LCDs are not prone to burn-in in the same way, which makes them a lower-risk choice for screens showing static content all day.

**Advantage:** LCD, for heavy static use.

## Motion and responsiveness

OLED pixels change state extremely quickly, which helps moving images look clear. That is one reason OLED has become popular for high-refresh-rate gaming monitors. Fast LCDs have also improved a great deal, so the gap depends on the model. Our explainer on [refresh rate, response time and input lag](/gaming/refresh-rate-response-time-input-lag/) covers the details.

**Advantage:** OLED, generally.

## Power and price

OLED power use depends on content: dark images use less power, bright full-screen images use more. LCD backlights use roughly the same power regardless of what is on screen.

On price, LCD remains widely available at every budget. OLED has become more affordable, but it often costs more at a given size.

## Side-by-side summary

| Factor | OLED | LCD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contrast and blacks | Excellent — pixels switch off | Good to very good with local dimming |
| Full-screen brightness | Often limited | Can be very high |
| Burn-in risk | Possible with heavy static content | Very low |
| Motion clarity | Excellent | Good to very good |
| Price | Often higher | Wide range, including budget options |

## Which should you choose?

- **Choose OLED** if you mostly watch films or play games in dim rooms, want the best contrast, and vary what is on screen.
- **Choose LCD** if your room is very bright, you display static content for many hours (such as office work or a dashboard), or you want the most screen for your money.
- **Consider mini-LED LCD** if you want high brightness *and* strong contrast, and you can accept some blooming.

## The verdict

Neither technology is simply "better". OLED wins on contrast and motion; LCD wins on sustained brightness, burn-in resistance and choice at lower prices. Decide based on your room, your content and your budget — then read reviews of the specific model, because implementation matters as much as the panel type.
