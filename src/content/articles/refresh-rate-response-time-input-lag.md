Gaming monitors are marketed with a familiar trio of numbers: a refresh rate in hertz, a response time in milliseconds and, sometimes, an input-lag figure. They are related, but they describe different things. Confusing them is one of the easiest ways to overspend — or to buy a monitor that disappoints.

## Refresh rate: how often the picture updates

**Refresh rate** is how many times per second the display draws a new image, measured in hertz (Hz). A 60 Hz monitor refreshes 60 times per second; a 144 Hz monitor, 144 times.

The time between refreshes is easy to calculate:

| Refresh rate | Time per refresh |
| --- | --- |
| 60 Hz | ≈ 16.7 ms |
| 120 Hz | ≈ 8.3 ms |
| 144 Hz | ≈ 6.9 ms |
| 240 Hz | ≈ 4.2 ms |
| 360 Hz | ≈ 2.8 ms |

Higher refresh rates make motion look smoother and can make games feel more responsive, because new information reaches your eyes sooner. The improvement from 60 Hz to 120 or 144 Hz is widely noticeable. Beyond that, returns diminish: each step up saves fewer milliseconds.

There is a catch: your computer or console must produce enough frames per second to benefit. A 240 Hz monitor showing a game running at 70 frames per second is not delivering 240 Hz motion.

## Variable refresh rate: keeping things in sync

When the game's frame rate and the monitor's refresh rate do not match, you can see **tearing** (parts of two frames on screen at once) or stutter. **Variable refresh rate (VRR)** technologies let the monitor adjust its refresh timing to match the frames being delivered. You will see them under names such as AMD FreeSync, NVIDIA G-SYNC and HDMI Forum VRR. Check that your graphics card or console supports the type your monitor offers.

## Response time: how fast pixels change

**Response time** measures how quickly a pixel changes from one shade to another, usually quoted in milliseconds as "grey-to-grey" (GtG). Slow pixel transitions leave smearing or ghosting behind moving objects.

Treat quoted response times with caution:

- Manufacturers often quote a best-case figure, sometimes measured with an aggressive "overdrive" setting.
- Overdrive pushes pixels to change faster but can cause **overshoot** — bright or dark halos around moving objects.
- Real-world response varies between different colour transitions.

Independent reviews that measure many transitions are more informative than the number on the box. As a general rule, response time should be comfortably shorter than the time per refresh; otherwise pixels are still changing when the next frame arrives.

OLED panels have very fast pixel transitions, which is one reason they perform well in motion. Our [OLED vs LCD comparison](/consumer-tech/oled-vs-lcd-displays/) explains the wider trade-offs.

## Input lag: the delay you feel

**Input lag** (or display latency) is the delay between the monitor receiving a frame and showing it. It is separate from response time: a monitor can have fast pixels but still add processing delay — for example from image-enhancement features.

Input lag is one part of a longer chain that also includes your mouse or controller, the game engine, the graphics card and the display. Reducing any one link helps, but total system latency is what you actually feel.

Practical tips:

- Use your monitor or TV's **game mode**, which typically disables processing that adds delay.
- Prefer independent latency measurements to marketing claims.
- Remember that a higher refresh rate also reduces latency by delivering frames more often.

## Which matters most?

It depends on what you play:

- **Competitive, fast-paced games:** high refresh rate, low input lag and clean motion matter most — provided your hardware can deliver high frame rates.
- **Story-driven and cinematic games:** image quality, contrast and HDR performance may matter more than going beyond 120–144 Hz.
- **Mixed use:** a good 120–165 Hz monitor with VRR is a sensible balance for many people.

## The bottom line

Refresh rate is how often the picture updates, response time is how quickly pixels can keep up, and input lag is how long the display takes to show what it receives. Look for a refresh rate your hardware can actually use, VRR support that matches your system, and independent measurements of motion and latency — not just the biggest numbers on the box.
