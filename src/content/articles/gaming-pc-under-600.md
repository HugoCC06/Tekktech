Building a gaming PC for less than £600 sounds difficult when graphics cards, memory and storage can consume a large part of the budget. Yet a carefully chosen budget system can still provide a useful entry point into PC gaming, particularly if you prioritise performance over appearance and avoid paying extra for features you do not need.

The important distinction is between building a complete computer for £600 and spending £600 on the tower alone. A monitor, keyboard, mouse, Windows licence and other accessories can push the total well beyond the target.

**A sub-£600 gaming PC is achievable in some circumstances, but the exact performance depends on component prices, availability and whether you buy new or used hardware.** There is no single parts list that will always offer the best value.

## What should a £600 budget include?

Before shopping, decide what the budget covers.

For this guide, assume the target is the computer tower, including the processor, graphics hardware, motherboard, RAM, storage, power supply and case. Peripherals are excluded.

That distinction matters. Someone who already owns a monitor and keyboard can put the entire budget towards the machine. Someone starting from scratch may need to reserve part of the money for those essentials.

It is also worth leaving some room for unexpected costs, delivery and any additional cooling or adapters required by the selected components.

## Where should the money go?

### The graphics card matters most for many games

For a gaming-focused desktop, the graphics card is often the most important component for performance at higher resolutions and graphical settings.

A dedicated GPU is generally preferable if your priority is modern 3D gaming. However, an integrated graphics solution can make sense for lighter games or as a temporary starting point if you plan to add a dedicated graphics card later.

Do not choose a graphics card based on its name alone. Compare independent benchmarks for the games you actually play, paying attention to resolution, settings, frame rates and available video memory.

### Choose a sensible processor

A modern entry-level processor can be sufficient for a budget gaming system. Spending heavily on the CPU while leaving too little for the graphics card can produce an unbalanced build.

The right processor also depends on your motherboard platform, upgrade plans and whether you intend to use the PC for other demanding tasks.

Compare total platform cost rather than processor price alone. A slightly cheaper CPU may require a more expensive motherboard or memory configuration.

### Avoid overspending on appearance

A glass-sided case, elaborate RGB lighting and a liquid-cooling system may look impressive, but they are not priorities for a budget gaming PC.

A basic case with adequate airflow is usually a more sensible choice. The same applies to premium motherboards, decorative accessories and oversized power supplies that do not serve a clear purpose.

Spend money where it affects performance, reliability and future upgrades.

These figures are spending targets rather than verified current prices. A suitable combination may require shifting money between categories, particularly if the graphics card or memory costs more than expected.

The power supply should never be selected purely by choosing the cheapest available unit. Buy a reputable model with suitable capacity, connectors and protections for the chosen components.

Likewise, check motherboard compatibility with the processor and memory before ordering. A seemingly affordable parts list can become more expensive if it requires additional adapters, a different case or other corrections.

## Should you buy a used graphics card?

Used hardware can make a budget build considerably more flexible. A graphics card that was expensive when new may offer attractive performance on the second-hand market.

However, used components introduce uncertainty. The seller may not provide a meaningful warranty, and a card's history may be difficult to verify.

Before buying, consider:

- Whether the seller allows testing or offers a return period.
- Whether the card has the correct power connectors and physical dimensions.
- Whether its performance is competitive with new alternatives at the same price.
- Whether the card's age, power consumption and remaining support suit your needs.

Avoid assuming that a used card is a bargain simply because its original retail price was high. Current performance and condition matter more than its launch price.

Buying a used graphics card while keeping the power supply, motherboard and other critical components reliable can be a reasonable compromise.

## What games can you expect to play?

There is no single performance figure for a £600 gaming PC. Results vary substantially according to the graphics card, processor, game engine and settings.

Broadly, a carefully balanced entry-level system can be suitable for competitive games, older releases and many less demanding titles. Modern graphically intensive games may require reduced settings, upscaling or compromises in resolution to achieve comfortable performance.

Competitive games such as esports titles often have different hardware demands from visually demanding open-world games. A system that performs well in one category may struggle in another.

Before buying components, search for benchmarks covering your target games and the exact graphics card and processor combination. Prioritise average frame rates, frame-time consistency and any limitations caused by insufficient graphics memory.

Do not rely exclusively on advertised performance figures or demonstrations using different hardware.

## Can you build it yourself, or should you buy prebuilt?

Building a PC gives you direct control over component selection and can make future upgrades easier. It also requires time, careful compatibility checking and some troubleshooting.

A prebuilt computer can offer a simpler purchase, a single warranty and less responsibility for diagnosing hardware faults. However, the specification, quality of the power supply and motherboard, and upgrade options deserve close inspection.

At this budget, compare complete systems rather than assuming that a custom build is always cheaper. Discounts on prebuilt machines can occasionally make them competitive with buying individual parts.

If you already have a suitable desktop, upgrading its graphics card or storage may offer better value than replacing the entire computer.

## What about Windows and peripherals?

A gaming PC is not the same as a complete gaming setup.

If you need a monitor, keyboard, mouse, headset and operating system licence, your actual spending could exceed £600. You may be able to reuse existing peripherals or find affordable alternatives, but these costs should be included in your planning.

Check the legitimacy and terms of any operating system licence. Avoid suspiciously cheap keys from unverified sellers, and do not assume that a computer includes a transferable licence simply because it previously ran Windows.

## The bottom line

Yes, a gaming PC for less than £600 can be realistic, especially if the budget covers the tower alone and you are flexible about component brands or used hardware.

The key is to prioritise the graphics card, choose a capable but affordable processor, use reliable supporting components and avoid spending too much on aesthetics. A sensible build can provide a useful gaming experience without requiring a premium budget.

However, £600 is a constraint, not a guarantee of a particular frame rate. Check current UK prices and independent benchmarks before committing to a parts list.

If your budget must also cover a monitor and peripherals, consider buying components gradually, reusing existing equipment or looking at discounted prebuilt systems. The best purchase is the one that delivers reliable performance within your actual budget.
